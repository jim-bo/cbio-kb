TL;DR

This single-institution case-control study from Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) looked at 64 men who developed rectal adenocarcinoma (READ) 5 or more years after radiotherapy (RT) for prostate cancer (PRAD). The paper calls these secondary rectal cancers (SRC; the page spells the term out to avoid confusion with the SRC gene) and compares them with primary rectal cancers (PRC) treated at MSKCC from 1994 to 2022 PMID:40100215. Patients with secondary rectal cancer were much older (median 78 vs 55 years). Their tumors were smaller, earlier-stage, and more often distal and on the anterior rectal wall. They received neoadjuvant therapy less often, and they had worse 5-year overall, disease-free, and distant recurrence-free survival than 1:1 propensity-matched men with PRC. After adjusting for treatment, secondary status was independently associated with worse disease-free survival (DFS) and distant recurrence-free survival (DRFS), but not with overall survival (OS). Targeted sequencing with MSK-IMPACT (31 secondary vs 541 primary tumors) showed lower tumor mutational burden (TMB), fewer APC and Wnt-pathway alterations, and more SMAD4 inactivation. Whole-exome sequencing (17 vs 28 tumors) showed more frameshift deletions, inframe deletions, and splice-site variants, but no COSMIC signature differed between groups. The authors suggest, as an untested hypothesis, that these patients might benefit from immunotherapy PMID:40100215.

Cohort & data

  • Setting: retrospective case-control study at MSKCC. Patients were treated between February 1, 1994 and “September 31, 2022” (date as printed). The study followed the STROBE reporting guideline and was approved by the institutional review board, with informed consent PMID:40100215.
  • How secondary cases were selected: 498 patients had colorectal adenocarcinoma and a history of prostate cancer. Of these, 64 had an adenocarcinoma 12 cm or less from the anal verge, diagnosed at least 5 years after RT for prostate cancer ended. These 64 were classified as secondary rectal cancer (100% male; median age 78 [IQR 72–82] years) PMID:40100215.
  • Prostate RT exposure (n=64):
    • Modality: external beam RT (EBRT) in 31 (48.4%), interstitial brachytherapy in 19 (29.7%), and both in 14 (21.9%).
    • Median dose: 7560 cGy (IQR 5040–7560) for EBRT and 14,400 cGy for brachytherapy.
    • Median latency from end of RT to rectal cancer diagnosis: 11.50 years (IQR 7.00–17.25).
    • Median follow-up after rectal cancer diagnosis: 2.38 years (IQR 1.97–3.26) PMID:40100215.
  • Primary rectal cancer controls:
    • 843 patients with rectal cancer and no history of prostate cancer or pelvic RT (231 female [27.4%], 612 male [72.6%]).
    • The clinical reference set is 604 male PRC patients (median age 55 [IQR 46–66] years). The text does not explain the difference between 612 and 604 men.
    • Outcomes were compared against 64 men with PRC, matched 1:1 by propensity score PMID:40100215.
  • Targeted sequencing:
    • Assay: MSK-IMPACT, described as covering “341 to 505 cancer-associated genes” (panel versions IMPACT341 through IMPACT505).
    • Samples: 31 secondary tumors, compared with 541 PRC tumors (223 female, 318 male) and with 62 PRC tumors matched 1:2 by propensity score. The genomic matching did not include sex.
    • Germline filtering for the 31 secondary tumors used matched blood for 24 (77.4%), adjacent normal tissue for 3 (9.7%), and pooled normal DNA for 4 (12.9%) PMID:40100215.
  • Whole-exome sequencing: 17 of the 31 secondary tumors (54.8%) had enough material for WES recapture, at ×150 depth for tumors and ×70 for normals (blood normals for 14 patients, adjacent non-cancer tissue for 3). They were compared with WES from 28 PRC tumors PMID:40100215.
  • Analysis pipeline:
    • TMB was defined as nonsynonymous mutations per megabase sequenced. Fraction of genome altered was defined using a |log2| copy-number threshold above 0.2.
    • Tools: FACETS for allele-specific copy number, MSIsensor for MSI (score threshold ≥10), OncoKB to classify variants as oncogenic, the MSKCC TEMPO pipeline for WES, and COSMIC v3 mutational signatures.
    • Statistics: R 4.3.2; Wilcoxon rank-sum and Fisher exact tests; Benjamini-Hochberg correction (significance at q<0.10); propensity score matching with the MatchIt nearest-neighbor method; Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank tests; and Cox models stratified by clinical stage PMID:40100215.
  • Propensity-matching variables: sex, age at rectal cancer diagnosis, stage, distance from the anal verge, tumor size, and circumferential tumor location. The 1:2 genomic matching used the same variables except sex PMID:40100215.
  • cBioPortal dataset: rectal_radiation_msk_2024. cBioPortal study metadata (not the paper) lists 48 rectal adenocarcinoma samples, WES plus MSK-IMPACT, on hg19. That number matches 31 IMPACT + 17 WES secondary-tumor samples, but this reading is an inference: the paper does not say which samples were deposited.

Treatment PMID:40100215

  • Neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) was given to 33/64 (51.6%) secondary vs 570/604 (94.4%) primary patients (P<.001).
  • Type of NAT differed (P<.001):
    • Secondary: chemotherapy only was the most common (19/33, 57.6%), total NAT 13 (39.4%), chemoradiation only 1 (3%).
    • Primary: total NAT 503 (88.2%), chemotherapy only 59 (10.4%), chemoradiation only 8 (1.4%).
  • Re-irradiation was not used before 2014 and became common afterwards. All 11 secondary patients who received NAT up to 2014 had chemotherapy only. After 2014, 14 of 22 (63.6%) received re-irradiation.
  • Among the 33 who received NAT:
    • 4 (12.1%) did not complete it.
    • 6 (18.2%) had grade 3 toxic effects, with no grade 4 or 5 events.
    • Watch-and-wait was offered at similar rates: 7/33 (21.2%) secondary vs 163/570 (28.6%) primary.
  • Among NAT recipients who had surgery, the type of operation differed (P<.001):
Procedure Secondary (of 12) Primary (of 341)
Abdominoperineal resection 6 (50.0%) 85 (24.9%)
Pelvic exenteration 2 (16.7%) 5 (1.5%)
Local excision 1 (8.3%) 12 (3.5%)
Low anterior resection 3 (25.0%) 239 (70.1%)
  • Among NAT recipients, 14 (42.4%) secondary vs 66 (11.6%) primary patients were not candidates for oncologic resection (“Other” category).

  • Stage IV patients were excluded from the DFS and DRFS curves.
  • In Cox models stratified by clinical stage and adjusted for neoadjuvant chemotherapy, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, and surgical resection, secondary status was associated with:
    • DFS: HR 6.16 (95% CI 2.10–18.07; P<.001).

  • Surgery was the only factor associated with OS (HR 0.38, 95% CI 0.16–0.88; P=.02). The Results text calls this “shorter OS”, which conflicts with an HR below 1 (see Limitations).

Targeted-sequencing genomics (31 secondary vs 541 PRC vs 62 matched PRC) PMID:40100215

  • The paper reports no MSI or POLE hypermutation among the profiled patients.
  • TMB was lower in secondary tumors (median 4.4 [IQR 3.2–6.7] mut/Mb) than in all PRC (5.8 [4.4–7.0]; P=.047) and matched PRC (6.1 [5.2–8.1]; P=.006).
  • There was no significant difference in fraction of genome altered, whole-genome duplication frequency, genome-wide copy-number patterns, or arm-level changes.

Genes & alterations

  • APC: inactivating somatic alterations were less frequent in secondary rectal cancer: 48.4% vs 79.9% in all PRC (P<.001) and 72.6% in matched PRC (P=.04). This lower APC rate drives the drop in Wnt-pathway alterations (54.8% vs 83.2%, P<.001). The authors note that APC wild-type status has been linked to more aggressive colorectal tumors, which could help explain the worse outcomes PMID:40100215.
  • SMAD4: inactivation was enriched in secondary rectal cancer: 25.8% vs 10.0% in all PRC (P=.01) and 8.1% in matched PRC (P=.03). The authors also cite SMAD4 alterations as a marker of more aggressive tumors PMID:40100215.
  • POLE: no POLE-hypermutant (or MSI) tumors were reported, so the differences in TMB and deletions are not explained by hypermutator phenotypes PMID:40100215.
  • Other rectal cancer driver genes altered in ≥10% of either cohort were compared (Figure 2D; eTables 4–5). The extracted text does not name any significant differences apart from APC and SMAD4 PMID:40100215.

Clinical implications

  • Prognosis: secondary rectal cancer after prostate RT carries a higher risk of distant recurrence (HR 12.68) and worse DFS (HR 6.16), independent of treatment differences. Local recurrence-free survival was similar to matched PRC PMID:40100215.
  • Treatment patterns: these patients were less likely to receive neoadjuvant chemoradiation, systemic chemotherapy, or curative-intent surgery. When they had surgery, they more often needed abdominoperineal resection or pelvic exenteration. Re-irradiation has become more accepted since 2014. Across all 33 NAT recipients (not only those re-irradiated), grade 3 toxic effects occurred in 18.2%, with no grade 4 or 5 events. Surgery was the only independent factor associated with OS PMID:40100215.
  • Immunotherapy hypothesis: frameshift indels can produce more immunogenic neoantigens. On that basis the authors “speculate that patients with [secondary rectal cancer] might be good candidates for immunotherapy.” They did not test this: the paper includes no immune checkpoint inhibitor outcome or immune-infiltrate data PMID:40100215.
  • Biologic distinctness: the authors read the clinical and genomic differences (lower TMB, APC wild-type, SMAD4 inactivation, excess deletions) as evidence that secondary rectal cancer is a biologically distinct entity. They do not treat it as sporadic rectal cancer arising in older men PMID:40100215.

Limitations & open questions

  • Stated by the authors:
    • Retrospective accrual over about 3 decades, with heterogeneous diagnosis, treatment, and surveillance of both prostate and rectal cancer.
    • Radiation dose, delivery, and modality changed over time.
    • Some “secondary” cases may be sporadic rectal cancers not caused by radiation.
    • The genomic analyses were small (31 IMPACT, 17 WES) and underpowered.
    • Independent validation is needed PMID:40100215.
  • Internal inconsistency: the Results say surgery was “the only factor associated with shorter OS (HR, 0.38 …)”. An HR below 1 means lower hazard, i.e., longer OS. The Discussion says only that surgery was “associated with OS”. Readers should interpret the direction from the HR PMID:40100215.
  • Follow-up vs endpoint: median follow-up for secondary cases was only 2.38 years (IQR 1.97–3.26), but the headline outcomes are 5-year Kaplan-Meier estimates. The OS gap is also confounded by a 23-year median age difference, although age was a matching variable in the outcome analysis PMID:40100215.
  • Sex imbalance in the genomic comparison: all secondary cases were men, while the 541 PRC comparators were 41.2% female. Sex was left out of the genomic matching because earlier rectal cancer profiling found no relevant sex differences PMID:40100215.
  • Anatomic confounding of APC (cross-paper): in the MSKCC rectal cohort of Chatila et al., APC/Wnt alteration frequency falls toward the anal verge (upper 92%, middle 90%, lower 77%; P<.001) PMID:35970919. Secondary tumors here were far more often distal (58.7% vs 22.5%), so some of the APC deficit could reflect location. The difference weakened but persisted against the 1:2 matched PRC set, which was matched on distance from the anal verge (48.4% vs 72.6%, P=.04) PMID:40100215. This is recorded here, not resolved.
  • Comparator provenance: the text does not say where the 541 PRC MSK-IMPACT and 28 PRC WES comparators came from. Whether they overlap with the MSKCC rectal cohorts of PMID:35970919 (rectal_msk_2022) is unknown. The reference list includes Chatila et al. PMID:35970919, Zehir et al. PMID:28481359, and Yaeger et al. PMID:29316426 among its MSK-IMPACT and colorectal genomics citations. It also cites the TCGA oncogenic signaling pathway framework PMID:29625050 for its pathway-level analysis.
  • No radiation-specific signature: the WES analysis reports only SBS signatures (SBS1, SBS6, SBS15), and none differed by group. The extracted text does not say whether indel (ID) signatures were tested, even though frameshift and inframe deletions were enriched. The deletion comparisons use raw per-tumor variant counts PMID:40100215.
  • Missing cohort detail: 612 male controls vs a 604-man PRC reference set is unexplained. The end date of the accrual window is given as the non-existent “September 31, 2022” PMID:40100215.
