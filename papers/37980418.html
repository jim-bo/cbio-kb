TL;DR

Zhang, Yang, Hu et al. profile the genomic landscape and tumor immune microenvironment of 408 solitary fibrous tumors (SFT) across a discovery cohort (SYSUCC, n=131) and three independent validation cohorts (FAHSYSU, CHCAMS cohort 1, CHCAMS cohort 2), using a 1021-gene targeted NGS panel and quantitative IHC/multiplex immunofluorescence. They identify an actionable IDH1 p.R132S hotspot mutation in 6.9% (9/131) of SFTs and a macrophage-dominant, PD-L1-high immune infiltrate in 24.4% (32/131) of cases as candidate targets for IDH1 inhibitors and combination immune/targeted therapy, and build a four-variable integrated risk model (mitotic count, Ki-67+ cell density, CD163+ cell density, MTOR mutation) that outperforms the WHO classification, the mDemicco model, and the G-score for predicting progression-free survival (PFS) in both primary non-CNS and CNS SFTs with negative tumor margins PMID:37980418.

Cohort & data

  • 408 histologically confirmed SFTs (SFT) across four cohorts: SYSUCC (discovery, n=131, resected 2008–2020, Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center), FAHSYSU (n=115, 2013–2021), CHCAMS cohort 1 (n=101, 2017–2021), CHCAMS cohort 2 (n=61, 2013–2016) — collectively the sft_sysucc_2023 study.
  • STAT6 IHC positivity (diagnostic surrogate for the NAB2–STAT6 fusion): 91.60% (120/131) SYSUCC, 98.26% (113/115) FAHSYSU, 96.04% (97/101) CHCAMS1, 98.36% (60/61) CHCAMS2; RT-PCR in 11 IHC-negative SYSUCC cases confirmed NAB2–STAT6 fusion transcripts in 5 more, bringing overall SYSUCC molecular/IHC confirmation to 95.42% (125/131).
  • Targeted NGS: the geneplus-1021-gene-panel (Geneplus-Beijing, hybrid-capture, ~1.1 Mb, GenePlus 2000 platform, 2×100bp paired-end, aligned to hg38 with bwa; SNVs/indels called with mutect and annotated with annovar; CNVs with CONTRA) was run on the SYSUCC discovery cohort (n=131) only.
  • Risk-model cohort: primary non-CNS SFTs with negative tumor margins (NTM) — SYSUCC discovery n=101; validation in FAHSYSU n=71, CHCAMS1 n=84, CHCAMS2 n=55 (pooled n=311 across all four cohorts); additionally validated in 36 primary CNS SFTs with NTM, and tested (unsuccessfully) in 31 relapsed non-CNS SFTs with NTM.
  • Immune profiling: multiplex IHC (STAT6, Ki-67, CD68, CD163, HLA-DPB1, PD-L1, CD3, CD4, CD8, FOXP3, CD11c, CD20) quantified with the HALO 2.3 digital pathology system on the SYSUCC cohort (n=131); multiplexed-immunofluorescence performed on 3 representative SFT cases to co-localize PD-L1 with macrophage/T-cell markers.
  • MTOR/IDH1 confirmation: sanger-sequencing used to validate MTOR mutations across all three validation cohorts (n=277) and IDH1 p.R132S in 7 SYSUCC specimens.

Key findings

  • Actionable IDH1 hotspot: IDH1 p.R132S was found in 6.9% (9/131) of SYSUCC SFTs by NGS, verified by Sanger sequencing. It was highly enriched in malignant-WHO-classification, high-nuclear-pleomorphism, and high-cellularity SFTs, but was not associated with PFS.
  • Genomic landscape: across the 1021-gene panel, the most frequently altered genes in the SYSUCC discovery cohort were ZFHX3 (25%), KMT2C (21%), TERT (19%), SLX4 (17%), FAT1 (16%), KMT2D (16%), ARID1B (14%), CDH23 (13%), and NOTCH1 (9%); 17.6% (23/131) of SFTs carried clinically actionable alterations.
  • Prognostic mutations: MTOR mutation (3.05–8.70% across cohorts) was associated with shorter PFS (p=3.00E-05, HR 8.31, 95% CI 0.36–189.53); TP53 mutation (7%, more common in malignant/male/high-mitotic-count SFTs) with shorter PFS (p=0.045, HR 2.81, 95% CI 0.56–14.09); ERCC5 mutation (4%) with shorter PFS (p=0.0024). TERT promoter mutations (19.08%, 25/131) were associated with malignant histology, necrosis, larger tumor size, and older age, but not with PFS in this cohort (p=0.18, HR 1.74, 95% CI 0.67–4.50). NOTCH-pathway alterations (NOTCH1, NOTCH2, NOTCH3, CREBBP) were also related to PFS.
  • Actionable low-frequency alterations mapped to drug classes (Fig. 2b): PARP-pathway gene alterations in 6.9% (9/131) — FANCF p.R284Lfs38, RAD54L* p.C179Wfs6, FANCA* p.H913Qfs29, FANCC* p.Y430, ATM* p.R2691C, RECQL4 p.V767Gfs42, NBN* p.R89, CHEK2* p.H371Y, WRN p.R732* — matched to PARP inhibitors (niraparib, olaparib, rucaparib, talazoparib); mTOR-pathway alterations in 3.1% (4/131) — PIK3CA p.E545A, NF2 c.675+1G>A, NF2 p.K253Rfs43, NF1* p.Q1086* — matched to mTOR inhibitors (everolimus, sirolimus, temsirolimus); one case (SFT-111, NRAS p.Q61K) within a 3-case (2.1%) RAS/MEK-pathway group was matched to the MEK inhibitors fcn-159 and pd0325901; single cases (0.8% each) of RB1 p.S318Nfs13 (CDK4/6-inhibitor target), PIK3CA* p.E545A (PI3K-inhibitor target), and BRAF p.G469R (BRAF-inhibitor / tyrosine-kinase-inhibitor target) were also flagged.
  • Immune microenvironment: tumor-infiltrating immune cells in SFTs were predominantly macrophages (median CD68+ 42.70 cells/mm², CD163+ 549.50 cells/mm², HLA-DPB1+ 2029.20 cells/mm²; Kruskal-Wallis p=2.20E-16 vs. lymphocyte populations). High density of CD163+ cells (p=0.0026, HR 2.93, 95% CI 1.32–6.52) and HLA-DPB1+ cells (p=2.50E-06, HR 4.93, 95% CI 1.99–12.18) were each independently associated with shorter PFS; CD68+ macrophage density was also significantly correlated with shorter PFS, whereas high CD20+ B-cell density was associated with longer PFS. Neither CD3+ nor CD8+ T-cell density was associated with PFS.
  • PD-L1 expression: 13.74% (18/131) of SFTs had high PD-L1 expression in tumor cells and 10.69% (14/131) in immune cells (chiefly CD68+/HLA-DPB1+/CD163+ macrophages); 24.4% (32/131) of SFTs showed high PD-L1 in either compartment. High PD-L1 in immune cells was associated with WHO classification, nuclear pleomorphism, and tumor site, but not with PFS. Two SFT cases had both IDH1 p.R132S and high PD-L1 expression.
  • Integrated risk model: LASSO regression and random survival forest selected four variables from clinical, histopathological, immunohistochemical, and molecular candidates: mitotic count (≥4/10 HPF), Ki-67+ cell density (≥454.7 cells/mm²), CD163+ cell density (≥929.3 cells/mm²), and MTOR mutation. Patients are scored into low (score=0), intermediate (0<score≤3.37), and high-risk (score>3.37) groups. In the SYSUCC discovery cohort (n=101) these groups comprised 62.38%/30.69%/6.93% of patients with 3-year PFS of 100%/89%/43% (p=7.49E-12); results were reproduced in FAHSYSU (3-year PFS 100%/95%/48%, p=4.81E-05), CHCAMS1 (3-year PFS 100%/75%/76%, p=0.039), and CHCAMS2 (5-year PFS 100%/83%/18%, p=3.30E-06).
  • Model performance vs. published models: the integrated model’s C-index and AUC exceeded those of the WHO classification, the mDemicco model, and the G-score in all four cohorts — e.g., in SYSUCC: C-index 0.911 (95% CI 0.846–0.976) vs. 0.787/0.857/0.855, and AUC 0.921 (95% CI 0.868–0.974) vs. 0.790/0.832/0.865, respectively. Pooled across all four cohorts (n=311), the integrated model stratified 50.80%/35.69%/13.50% of patients into low/intermediate/high risk with 5-year PFS of 100%/73%/21% (p=2.00E-16), while the comparator models showed poor inter-model correlation and less-consistent stratification. In the 36-case CNS SFT/NTM cohort, the integrated model’s C-index (0.901) and AUC (0.867) exceeded WHO grade (0.802 and 0.756, respectively). The model did not stratify the 31-case relapsed non-CNS SFT/NTM cohort.

Genes & alterations

  • NAB2–STAT6: the defining SFT gene fusion; used diagnostically via STAT6 nuclear IHC (positive in 91.6–98.4% of cases across the four cohorts) rather than direct DNA/RNA fusion detection, because the panel used lacked RNA-based NGS.
  • IDH1 p.R132S: 6.9% (9/131) of SYSUCC SFTs; enriched in malignant, high-pleomorphism, high-cellularity tumors; not prognostic for PFS; proposed actionable target for IDH1 inhibitors (ivosidenib, vorasidenib).
  • MTOR: mutated in 3.05–8.70% of SFTs across the four cohorts; associated with shorter PFS (p=3.00E-05) and incorporated as the sole molecular variable in the integrated risk model.
  • TP53: 7% mutated; associated with shorter PFS (p=0.045) and more common in malignant, male, high-mitotic-count SFTs.
  • ERCC5: 4% mutated; associated with shorter PFS (p=0.0024).
  • TERT promoter: 19.08% (25/131) mutated; associated with malignant histology, necrosis, large tumor size, and older age, but not PFS.
  • NOTCH pathway (NOTCH1, NOTCH2, NOTCH3, CREBBP): alterations related to PFS.
  • DNA-repair/PARP-pathway genes (FANCF, RAD54L, FANCA, FANCC, ATM, RECQL4, NBN, CHEK2, WRN): 6.9% (9/131) combined, proposed PARP-inhibitor targets.
  • mTOR-pathway genes (PIK3CA, NF2, NF1): 3.1% (4/131) combined, proposed mTOR-inhibitor targets in addition to MTOR itself.
  • NRAS p.Q61K: within a 3-case (2.1%) RAS/MEK-pathway group proposed for MEK-inhibitor targeting.
  • RB1 p.S318Nfs*13 and BRAF p.G469R: single cases (0.8% each) flagged as CDK4/6-inhibitor and BRAF-inhibitor/tyrosine-kinase-inhibitor targets, respectively.
  • Most frequently altered genes (frequency, not necessarily prognostic): ZFHX3 (25%), KMT2C (21%), TERT (19%), SLX4 (17%), FAT1 (16%), KMT2D (16%), ARID1B (14%), CDH23 (13%), NOTCH1 (9%).

Clinical implications

  • IDH1-directed therapy: the authors argue IDH1 p.R132S is a tractable target given approved IDH1 inhibitors (ivosidenib) in IDH1-mutant cholangiocarcinoma and acute myeloid leukemia, and propose extending IDH1/2 inhibition (including vorasidenib) as an additional treatment option for high-grade SFTs.
  • Combination immunotherapy: given predominant macrophage infiltration, PD-L1 positivity concentrated in CD68+/HLA-DPB1+/CD163+ macrophages, and reported activity of anti-angiogenics (e.g., pazopanib, cited from prior ESMO-EURACAN-GENTURIS-endorsed phase II trials with ~50% overall response rate) and CSF1R inhibition in other tumor types, the authors propose biomarker-guided combinations of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 blockade with anti-angiogenic, anti-macrophage (CSF1R-inhibitor), or anti-IDH1 therapy as a future treatment strategy for SFTs — extrapolating in part from a published nivolumab-sunitinib combination trial in other soft-tissue sarcomas.
  • Risk stratification: the four-variable integrated model (mitotic count, Ki-67+ density, CD163+ density, MTOR mutation) is proposed to guide surveillance intensity and treatment aggressiveness for both primary non-CNS and CNS SFTs with negative tumor margins, outperforming the WHO classification/grade, mDemicco model, and G-score on C-index and AUC in all tested cohorts. Incorporation of MTOR mutation status is described as increasingly feasible given the growing routine use of targeted NGS testing.
  • Not validated for relapsed disease: the model failed to stratify relapsed (recurrent/metastatic) non-CNS SFTs with NTM (n=31), so its clinical use is currently limited to primary tumors with negative margins.

Limitations & open questions

  • No matched normal DNA was sequenced, which the authors note could have improved mutation-detection specificity.
  • The targeted panel lacked RNA-based NGS, so some fusion genes — notably NAB2–STAT6 itself — could not be directly detected at the sequence level; diagnosis relied on STAT6 IHC (plus RT-PCR follow-up in IHC-negative cases).
  • Most cohort specimens were resected within the past several years, so long-term follow-up outcomes are not yet available.
  • The integrated risk model did not significantly stratify relapsed (recurrent/metastatic) non-CNS SFTs with NTM (n=31), which the authors attribute to the added biological complexity of relapsed disease.
  • The authors state the practical clinical implications of the model (surveillance intensity, treatment escalation) still require prospective evaluation.
  • Figure 2b presents a detailed case-by-case mapping of low-frequency actionable mutations to specific drug classes; several rows of this figure (including a PIK3CA/AKT-pathway group of 2 cases, 1.5%) could not be reliably transcribed from the extracted PDF text due to OCR column-interleaving artifacts — readers should consult the original figure for the complete matrix.
  • No prospective or retrospective clinical outcomes with any of the proposed targeted/immune therapies (IDH1, PARP, mTOR, MEK, or PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors) are reported in this study; the therapeutic proposals are based on mutation-drug matching rather than treatment response data.
