TL;DR

Papaemmanuil, Campbell and colleagues, for the ICGC Chronic Myeloid Disorders Working Group, sequenced 111 cancer genes in 738 patients with myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) or closely related neoplasms, including chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) and MDS/MPN. They used tumor-only, whole-genome-amplified DNA with no matched germline. Combining sequencing with cytogenetics, 78% of patients had at least one oncogenic lesion. Oncogenic mutations were found in 43 genes, led by SF3B1 (24%), TET2 (22%) and SRSF2 (14%). The authors found 46 significant pairwise gene–gene associations, with co-occurrence and mutual exclusivity centered on spliceosome genes and epigenetic modifiers. Using allele fractions, they inferred that splicing and DNA-methylation mutations occur early and chromatin and signaling mutations occur later. This led them to propose a hypothesis of genetic “predestination”, in which early driver mutations, typically in RNA-splicing genes, shape the later trajectory of clonal evolution. Clonal and subclonal driver mutations carried the same prognostic weight. Leukemia-free survival fell steadily as the number of driver lesions rose, from a median of 49 months with 1 lesion to 4 months with ≥6. This effect held after accounting for IPSS (PMID:24030381).

Cohort & data

  • N = 738 patients sequenced; 595 had outcome data; median follow-up 12 months (range 0–155). 415 (56%) were male, and mean age was 68 ± 13 years (PMID:24030381).
  • Disease classification (WHO 2008, except that RCMD-RS was kept as a separate WHO 2002 category). Counts are from Table 1:
    • RA: 139 (19%)
    • RARS: 92 (12%)
    • RARS-T: 17 (2%) (MDSMPNRST)
    • RCMD: 126 (17%)
    • RCMD-RS: 59 (8%)
    • RAEB: 167 (23%) (MDSEB)
    • 5q-: 20 (3%) (MDSID5Q)
    • CMML: 70 (9%)
    • MDS-MPN: 3 (0.4%)
    • MDS-U: 10 (1%)
    • MDS-AML (progressive disease, AML): 35 (5%)
    RARS and RCMD-RS together make up the ring-sideroblast MDS group (MDSRS). Overall, 603 patients had MDS proper (PMID:24030381).
  • Baseline features:
    • Hemoglobin 9.9 ± 2.0 g/dL, neutrophils 3.0 ± 3.4 ×10⁹/L, platelets 191 ± 181 ×10⁹/L.
    • Ring sideroblasts >15% in 173/529 (33%); marrow blasts >5% in 202 (27%).
    • Karyotype normal in 425, abnormal in 213, and failed or not done in 100.
    • During follow-up, 66/595 (11%) transformed to leukemia and 234/595 (39%) died (PMID:24030381).
  • DNA source: peripheral-blood granulocytes (n = 431) or bone-marrow mononuclear cells (n = 307). Germline DNA was generally not available (PMID:24030381).
  • Assay:
    • All DNA underwent whole-genome-amplification.
    • An RNA-bait capture panel of 111 genes was then sequenced (targeted-dna-seq), using 96-well barcoded libraries on 2 lanes of an Illumina HiSeq. Genes were chosen for prior implication in myeloid disease, recurrence in common cancers, in-house candidate status, or location in common copy-number-altered regions.
    • Calling used in-house algorithms against an unrelated reference sample, followed by a custom stringent variant-selection pipeline. This yielded 2260 high-confidence variants.
    • Variants were then triaged into “driver mutations”, “possible oncogenic” or “unknown significance”, based on literature-defined expected mutation patterns (PMID:24030381).
  • Cytogenetics: conventional bone-marrow karyotyping. Copy-number profiles were also derived from sequencing read counts (PMID:24030381).
  • Validation controls (6 datasets):
    • Matched tumor/constitutional whole-exome-seq for 10 WGA samples.
    • 18 technical replicates.
    • Prior SF3B1 and TET2 calls.
    • WGA plus panel sequencing of 22 normal DNAs.
    • The same pipeline applied to 56 normal blood exomes.
    • Exome or whole-genome data from 317 constitutional DNA samples (PMID:24030381).
  • Statistics:
    • Pairwise associations: Fisher tests with multiple-testing correction.
    • Univariate tests: log-rank on leukemia-free survival.
    • Multivariate models: cox-proportional-hazards models with multiple imputation of missing data and stability selection. Variables were selected with lasso-regression, and accuracy was averaged over 5-fold cross-validation.
    • Clonal relationships: Pearson goodness-of-fit tests on variant allele fractions, followed by phylogenetic-tree-reconstruction. Global temporal ordering used a bradley-terry-model (PMID:24030381).
  • Data deposition: sequence data were deposited at the European Genome-Phenome Archive. No accession is given in the article text (PMID:24030381).
  • cBioPortal dataset:
    • This paper is one of three publications cited by the cBioPortal study mds_mskcc_2020 (“Myelodysplastic (MSK, 2020)”; 4231 samples; hg19). The other two are PMID:27276561 (Papaemmanuil et al., NEJM 2016) and PMID:30333627 (Tyner et al., Nature 2018).
    • The portal sample count therefore does not correspond to this paper’s 738-patient cohort.

Key findings

  • Validation of tumor-only calling:
    • In 10 patients with matched exomes, all 21 somatic variants in the 111 genes were detected and 20/21 passed filters (95.2% sensitivity). Every variant annotated as a driver was confirmed somatic.
    • No oncogenic variants were called in 78 constitutional samples (22 WGA-screened normals plus 56 normal blood exomes).
    • All 147 previously known SF3B1 mutations were recovered, plus 11 that had been missed earlier because of poor coverage. For TET2, 20/21 known mutations (95%) were recovered in 184 patients, plus 3 additional ones.
    • WGA did not significantly distort variant allele fractions (PMID:24030381).
  • Mutational landscape:
    • Oncogenic mutations were found in 43 genes, led by SF3B1 (24%), TET2 (22%) and SRSF2 (14%).
    • Only 4 genes were mutated in >10% of patients and 3 more in 5–10%. The remaining 36 genes were each mutated in <5% of patients, but together they contributed 33.5% of all mutations (PMID:24030381).
  • Copy number from sequencing:
    • Credible copy-number profiles were obtained for 629/738 patients (85%), and abnormalities were detected in 101 (13%).
    • Detected changes included deletions of 5q, 11q, 20q and 17p, monosomy 7, trisomies 8 and 21, and isochromosome X.
    • Sequencing also found uniparental disomy that cytogenetics cannot see (PMID:24030381).
  • Driver yield:
    • 549/738 patients (74%; 95% CI 71–77%) had ≥1 oncogenic point mutation or MDS-related copy-number change detectable by sequencing. Cytogenetics alone found abnormalities in 33%, and sequencing plus cytogenetics raised the yield to 78%.
    • 43% of patients had 2–3 oncogenic lesions, and 10% had 4–8 (PMID:24030381).
  • Gene–gene interactions: 46 gene pairs were significant at FDR <10% (PMID:24030381).
    • Spliceosome-gene mutations were mutually exclusive, and TET2 and IDH2 were mutually exclusive.
    • Some exclusive pairs cannot be explained by redundancy within one pathway. EZH2 and SRSF2 mutations were never found together (q = 0.04), and IDH2 was mutually exclusive with SF3B1.
    • By contrast, IDH2 was strongly co-mutated with SRSF2 (OR 6.7, 95% CI 4.9–9.3; q = 0.0004).
    • SF3B1 and SRSF2 had strikingly different sets of co-mutated genes. SF3B1 was linked to ring-sideroblast MDS (MDSRS), while SRSF2 was enriched in CMML.
  • Clonal architecture: among 313 patients with ≥2 drivers (PMID:24030381):
    • 62% had only clonal drivers.
    • In 4%, the subclonal fractions were too low to resolve phylogeny.
    • 34% had strong evidence of both clonal and subclonal drivers, which allowed pairwise precedences to be defined. 107 patients contributed informative precedences.
  • Temporal ordering:
    • A Bradley–Terry ranking, with ASXL1 as the reference point, showed that RNA-splicing and DNA-methylation mutations occur early. Chromatin-modification and signaling mutations tend to occur later.
    • These were trends rather than absolute rules. The authors proposed the genetic “predestination” hypothesis on this basis (PMID:24030381).
  • Single-gene prognosis:
    • Of 24 genes mutated in >5 patients, 8 were associated with significantly worse leukemia-free survival (LFS). One gene, SF3B1, was associated with better LFS (supplemental Figure 8; the 8 adverse genes are not listed in the main text).
    • These results replicated prior studies (PMID:24030381).
  • Clonal = subclonal for prognosis: six genes with published survival effects had ≥5 patients carrying subclonal mutations: TET2, ASXL1, SRSF2, EZH2, CBL and RUNX1. For all six, LFS did not differ significantly between clonal and subclonal mutations (PMID:24030381).
  • Driver number predicts outcome:
    • LFS worsened with the combined number of oncogenic mutations and cytogenetic lesions (P < 0.0001). The trend held with gene mutations only (P = 0.002) and remained significant independent of TP53 or SF3B1 status.
    • Median LFS was 49, 42, 27, 18 and 4 months for 1, 2, 3, 4–5 and ≥6 lesions. The rate of transformation to acute leukemia rose monotonically with driver number (P < 0.0001).
    • Driver count added prognostic information after stratification by IPSS (P = 0.0004) (PMID:24030381).
  • Driver-negative patients:
    • 22% of patients had no known oncogenic point mutation or cytogenetic aberration. Their LFS and transformation rates resembled those of patients with 1–2 drivers.
    • DNA source (marrow vs. blood) did not systematically affect the driver distribution (PMID:24030381).
  • Prognostic modeling (ipss vs. genomics):
    • The time-dependent AUC at 90 months was 0.76 for IPSS and 0.80 for all standard clinical variables. Adding point mutations gave 0.82, a marginal and nonsignificant gain.
    • The standard and genomic curves broadly overlapped, which suggests the two carry partly redundant prognostic information.
    • IPSS-R could not be calculated because of missing data (PMID:24030381).
  • Genotype–phenotype:
    • TET2 mutations (positive correlation) and del(5q) (negative correlation) were the strongest genetic predictors of hemoglobin.
    • WT1, IDH2, STAG2 and NRAS mutations, and complex karyotype, correlated strongly with marrow blast percentage. SF3B1 predicted a low blast fraction (PMID:24030381).
  • Long tail of rare drivers: 20% of mutations in patients with a single driver fell in genes mutated in <2% of cases. The same was true for 30–40% of additional (3rd, 4th, and later) mutations (PMID:24030381).

Genes & alterations

  • SF3B1:
    • Most frequently mutated gene (24%) and an early event.
    • The only gene associated with better LFS.
    • Mutually exclusive with SRSF2 and IDH2. Linked to ring sideroblasts (MDSRS) and predicts a low marrow blast fraction.
    • Its co-mutation partners differ sharply from those of SRSF2 (PMID:24030381).
  • TET2:
    • Mutated in 22%; an early DNA-methylation-pathway driver.
    • Mutually exclusive with IDH2, which the authors link to shared disordered DNA hydroxymethylation.
    • The strongest positive genetic predictor of hemoglobin. Clonal and subclonal mutations carried equivalent LFS risk (PMID:24030381).
  • SRSF2:
    • Mutated in 14%; an early splicing driver, enriched in CMML.
    • Strongly co-mutated with IDH2 (OR 6.7). Never co-mutated with EZH2 (q = 0.04) and mutually exclusive with SF3B1.
    • Clonal and subclonal mutations had equivalent LFS (PMID:24030381).
  • ASXL1:
    • Reference point for the temporal-ordering model. Clonal and subclonal mutations had equivalent LFS.
    • 10 variants at previously uncharacterized residues (classified VUS) marked significantly worse LFS than ASXL1-wild-type cases (P = 0.03, log-rank), tracking the curve for known oncogenic ASXL1 mutations.
    • The authors cite an Asxl1-loss plus Nras mouse model that produced more aggressive disease, as support for the observed ASXL1–NRAS co-mutation (PMID:24030381).
  • IDH2:
    • Co-mutated with SRSF2, and mutually exclusive with TET2 and SF3B1.
    • Correlated with higher marrow blast percentage (PMID:24030381).
  • EZH2: a chromatin-modifier driver that is never co-mutated with SRSF2. Clonal and subclonal mutations had equivalent LFS (PMID:24030381).
  • CBL and RUNX1: both had published survival effects, and for each, clonal and subclonal mutations carried equivalent LFS risk (PMID:24030381).
  • WT1, STAG2, NRAS: mutations correlated strongly with marrow blast percentage. NRAS is co-mutated with ASXL1 (PMID:24030381).
  • TP53: the prognostic effect of driver number remained significant independent of TP53 mutation status (PMID:24030381).
  • IRF1, CUX1:
    • Oncogenic IRF1 mutations were found; the group had previously seen IRF1 in 1 RARS patient.
    • CUX1, recently reported in AML, was also mutated in this cohort (PMID:24030381).
  • EP300, CREBBP, PTEN: well-known cancer genes not previously implicated in MDS. Mutations were rare (<2%) but showed the nonsense, splice-site and frameshift pattern typical of other cancers (PMID:24030381).
  • SETBP1, SMC1A, SMC3: not on the 111-gene bait set, because they were described after it was designed. The authors suggest these genes could explain part of the 22% of driver-negative patients (PMID:24030381).
  • Cytogenetic lesions:
    • del(5q) was the strongest negative genetic predictor of hemoglobin.
    • Complex karyotype correlated with high blast percentage.
    • Uniparental disomy was detectable from sequencing data alone (PMID:24030381).

Clinical implications

  • Prognosis: the number of driver mutations plus cytogenetic lesions is one of the strongest predictors of LFS and of transformation to AML. It adds independent information beyond IPSS (P = 0.0004) (PMID:24030381).
  • Subclonal detection matters: subclonal drivers carried the same LFS risk as clonal drivers in 6 prognostic genes. The authors therefore argue that detecting subclonal mutations could identify high-risk patients early. They also propose sequencing sequential blood samples, for example annually, to catch emerging subclones before higher-risk disease becomes clinically apparent (PMID:24030381).
  • Panel breadth: many drivers are rare. A sizeable share of mutations fall in genes mutated in <2% of patients, so diagnostic screening should sequence a comprehensive set of well-characterized genes (PMID:24030381).
  • Tumor-only clinical sequencing is feasible: the authors present this as the first targeted resequencing of a clinical cohort without matched constitutional DNA. With a stringent pipeline, driver calls were accurate (PMID:24030381).
  • Genomic prognostic scores need scale: adding mutations to standard variables raised the AUC only marginally (0.80 to 0.82, not significant). The authors estimate that an IPSS-R-scale cohort (IPSS-R used 7012 patients) may be needed for robust genomically informed prognostic schemes (PMID:24030381).
  • No drug-response findings: patients were censored when they started disease-modifying therapy (allogeneic transplant, aggressive chemotherapy, or hypomethylating agents). No drug-response associations were analyzed (PMID:24030381).

Limitations & open questions

  • No matched germline DNA: germline polymorphisms could be misannotated as drivers. Validation controls (95.2% sensitivity; no oncogenic calls in 78 constitutional samples) mitigate but do not eliminate this risk (PMID:24030381).
  • Whole-genome amplification could represent mutations non-uniformly, introduce artifacts, or distort allele burden. Control data showed no significant VAF bias (PMID:24030381).
  • “Predestination” is a hypothesis: it rests on cross-sectional inference, and the authors state that it needs longitudinal analysis of serial samples from large cohorts (PMID:24030381).
  • Conservative annotation: many variants were left as “unknown significance”. The ASXL1 VUS analysis is exploratory; confirming these variants would require showing they are somatic, recurrent and independently prognostic (PMID:24030381).
  • Panel gaps: the bait set predates SETBP1, SMC1A and SMC3. The 22% driver-negative fraction may therefore be overstated (PMID:24030381).
  • Clinical data gaps:
    • IPSS-R could not be calculated because of missing data.
    • Missing covariates were multiply imputed.
    • Ring-sideroblast data were missing for 209 patients.
    • Median follow-up was only 12 months (PMID:24030381).
  • Copy-number calling from panel reads is preliminary: the authors suggest adding germline SNP targets to increase sensitivity (PMID:24030381).
  • Internal count discrepancy: the Methods text says 13 patients were “of undefined MDS category or classified as MDS-MPN (including RARS-T)”. Table 1 lists RARS-T 17, MDS-MPN 3 and MDS-U 10, which sum to 30 and give the 738 total (PMID:24030381).
  • Cross-paper synthesis:
    • The authors justify tumor-only driver calling by pointing to the well-established somatic landscape of large genomics studies, including TCGA AML (PMID:23634996). Their in-house calling algorithms were previously described in the Sanger breast-cancer study (PMID:22722201).
    • The early-splicing and early-epigenetic ordering here can be compared with the clonal-ordering and chromatin–spliceosome findings in AML from the same group (PMID:27276561).
