A new genomic framework to categorize pediatric acute myeloid leukemia

TL;DR

The St. Jude group profiled 887 pediatric acute myeloid leukemia (pAML) cases by RNA-seq (all cases) plus orthogonal WGS/WES (74.9% of cases), integrating fusion/SV calling, SNV/indel calling, CNV and expression clustering to define 23 mutually exclusive molecular categories covering 91.4% of the cohort — versus 68.5% classifiable under the WHO 5th-edition schema. Twelve of the 23 categories (including UBTF, GLIS-family (GLISr), GATA1, BCL11B, MECOM, MNX1, KAT6A rearrangements (KAT6Ar), PICALM::MLLT10, RUNX1::RUNX1T1-like, CBFB-GDXY, FET-ETS fusions (ETS family) and KMT2A-PTD) are not defined in WHO5th. Categories clustered into HOXA- and HOXB-expression “superfamilies” with distinct cooperating-mutation patterns (KRAS vs. FLT3-ITD/WT1, respectively). Using the independent COG AAML1031 (n = 1,034) and St. Jude AML08 (n = 221) cohorts, the authors show these molecular categories are strongly associated with outcome and combine them with end-of-induction minimal residual disease (MRD) status into a six-stratum risk framework (PMID:38212634).

Cohort & data

  • 887 unique pediatric AML cases (age 0–23.5 years, median 9.3), collected from prior published cohorts and newly sequenced St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital cases; 783 (88.3%) at diagnosis and 104 (11.7%) at relapse (PMID:38212634).
  • Cancer type: AML (pediatric acute myeloid leukemia).
  • Discovery cohort profiled by RNA-seq for fusion, ITD/PTD, CNV, SNV and indel calling in all 887 cases; 665 cases (74.9%) also had orthogonal whole-genome sequencing (59.2%) or whole-exome sequencing (44.0%), which validated 97.3% of RNA-seq-derived calls.
  • Validation/outcome cohorts: the Children’s Oncology Group AAML1031 trial (NCT01371981, n = 1,034, RNA-seq reprocessed with the same pipeline) and the St. Jude AML08 trial (NCT00703820, n = 221, independent of AAML1031).
  • Data sources contributing to the discovery cohort include TARGET-AML (phs000218/phs000465, n = 187 of the 887) alongside prior St. Jude and collaborator cohorts (Supplementary Table 1).
  • cBioPortal study id: aml_stjude_2024.

Key findings

  • 627/887 patients (70.7%) had a pathogenic fusion/structural variant; the most common were KMT2A rearrangements (KMT2Ar, 20.3%) and RUNX1::RUNX1T1 (12.4%) (PMID:38212634).
  • 1,924 pathogenic/likely pathogenic somatic mutations were found in 749/887 patients (84.4%), including class-defining NPM1 (7.6%) and CEBPA (5.5%) mutations; RAS-pathway mutations were the single most frequent event class (37.5%, 333/887), with 21.3% of those (71/333) harboring more than one RAS-pathway mutation.
  • The final classification comprises 23 mutually exclusive molecular categories covering 91.4% of the cohort, versus 68.5% classifiable by WHO5th; 12 of the 23 categories are new relative to WHO5th.
  • Two large HOX-expression “superfamilies” emerged from clustering: the HOXA group (KMT2Ar and KAT6Ar, ~18.5% of the cohort) and the HOXB group (NPM1, NUP98r, UBTF, KMT2A-PTD, DEK::NUP214, ~23.3% of the cohort). FLT3-ITD accounted for 67.5% of FLT3-ITD+ cases in the HOXB group, while FLT3-TKD predominated in the HOXA group; KRAS mutations were enriched in HOXA (20.7% vs. 3.9% in HOXB) and WT1 mutations were enriched in HOXB (57.6%).
  • In the AAML1031 validation cohort (n = 1,034), recursive partitioning on molecular category (and, for KMT2Ar, fusion partner) produced three risk groups (low n = 499, intermediate n = 186, high n = 349) with distinct overall survival (log-rank P = 9.8 × 10⁻¹⁹); age and FLT3-ITD were not independently prognostic in this cohort, which the authors attribute to sorafenib being given to high-allelic-ratio FLT3-ITD+ patients on AAML1031.
  • MRD positivity at end of induction I was independently prognostic (P = 1.3 × 10⁻⁴¹) and, combined with the three risk groups, generated six risk strata with distinct overall survival (P = 1.5 × 10⁻³⁷ for the low-risk MRD− vs. MRD+ comparison shown in Fig. 7f); this prognostic framework was validated in the independent AML08 cohort (n = 221).
  • 76 “Unclassified” cases (8.6% of the cohort) lacked a category-defining alteration: 21 carried rare recurrent drivers (e.g., RUNX1 fusions to USP42/EVX1/ZEB2, MLLT10 fusions), 9 had no pathogenic alteration detected (partly reflecting missing WGS for 8/9), and the remainder were enriched for ETV6, RUNX1, TP53 and myelodysplasia-related-gene mutations plus complex karyotype/monosomy 7.

Genes & alterations

  • KMT2A — rearrangements (KMT2Ar, 180/887, 20.3%) and partial tandem duplications (KMT2A-PTD, 14 cases, assigned to its own molecular category); the largest single class-defining group, clustering in the HOXA superfamily along with KAT6Ar (PMID:38212634).
  • RUNX1::RUNX1T1 fusion — 110 cases (12.4%); a favorable-outcome CBF-AML category with high granulocyte-monocyte progenitor (GMP) scores.
  • CBFB::MYH11 fusion — 96 cases; favorable-outcome CBF-AML category. A distinct CBFB-GDXY insertion (CBFB, 4 cases) defines a separate, also favorable-outcome category.
  • NPM1 — mutation or in-frame fusion, 76 cases (7.6% of mutation-positive cases); cases with NPM1 fusions or non-C-terminal indels were clustered into the NPM1 category by expression similarity. Low GMP score and mid-high stemness score distinguished NPM1 cases despite an otherwise favorable-prognosis expression pattern.
  • NUP98 rearrangements (NUP98r, 71 cases), frequently cryptic (e.g., NUP98::NSD1); part of the HOXB superfamily with strong FLT3-ITD and WT1 co-occurrence.
  • UBTF tandem duplications — 50 cases; a cytogenetically cryptic, mutation-only defining lesion not represented in WHO5th; HOXB-superfamily expression pattern similar to NPM1 and DEK::NUP214.
  • CEBPA — biallelic or bZIP-domain mutations, 49 cases; favorable-outcome category with high GMP scores.
  • GLIS-family rearrangements (GLISr, 28 cases), primarily CBFA2T3::GLIS2; associated with poor outcome.
  • DEK::NUP214 fusion — 16 cases; HOXB superfamily; showed a favorable outcome in the AAML1031 validation cohort, contrasting with its historically poor-prognosis reputation.
  • FET-ETS family fusions (ETS family, 16 cases), e.g. FUS::ERG.
  • BCL11B structural variants with outlier expression — 13 cases; clusters with other “immature” categories (MECOM, MNX1, ETS family, PICALM::MLLT10).
  • MECOM structural variants with outlier expression — 10 cases; associated with poor outcome, consistent with prior adult-AML MECOM literature.
  • RBM15::MRTFA fusion — 10 cases.
  • KAT6A rearrangements (KAT6Ar, 7 cases); HOXA-superfamily, expression pattern similar to KMT2Ar.
  • PICALM::MLLT10 fusion — 8 cases; associated with poor outcome and frequent complex karyotype.
  • MNX1 structural variants with outlier expression — 5 cases (t(7;12)-type).
  • Acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL, 4 cases): PML::RARA fusions, plus one TBL1XR1::RARB fusion assigned by expression similarity to canonical PML::RARA cases.
  • RUNX1::RUNX1T1-like (3 cases): RUNX1::CBFA2T2/3 fusions.
  • BCR::ABL1 fusion — 1 case.
  • GATA1 mutation or fusion (e.g., MYB::GATA1) with acute erythroid leukemia (AEL)/acute megakaryoblastic leukemia (AMKL) phenotype — 23 cases; a Group-2 category defined only in the absence of Group-1 alterations.
  • HOX-cluster gene rearrangements (HOXr) with AEL/AMKL phenotype — 17 cases; another Group-2 category, frequently complex karyotype.
  • Cooperating/pathway mutations: FLT3-ITD (151), FLT3-TKD (53) and FLT3-non-TKD (29); WT1 (115); NRAS (188) and KRAS (78) as the dominant RAS-pathway mutations, with distinct enrichment by HOXA/HOXB group; NF1 and PTPN11 among other RAS-pathway genes; TP53 (24, largely in myelodysplasia-related/Unclassified cases); ETV6 and RUNX1 loss-of-function alterations enriched in the immature/Unclassified subset; epigenetic modifiers IDH1, IDH2, ASXL1, BCOR, KDM6A and PHF6 differentially enriched between HOXA and HOXB groups by GRIN analysis; RB1 focal deletions (13q14, 2.9%) identified by GISTIC.
  • Rare, single-case candidate drivers in Unclassified cases: high-allelic-burden JAK2 p.V617F, MLLT1 p.C119SPAR (resembling the recurrent MLLT1 hotspot in Wilms tumor) and H3-3A (H3F3A) p.K28M (resembling the recurrent H3-3A hotspot in high-grade glioma), proposed as candidate drivers pending confirmation in a larger cohort.

Clinical implications

  • The authors propose combining molecular category assignment with end-of-induction MRD status as a new pAML risk-stratification framework; predictive performance was comparable to or better than current pAML trial risk stratifications and to the adult ELN2022 classification when applied to their cohorts (PMID:38212634).
  • Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in first remission showed a benefit for high-risk, MRD-positive categories; benefit for the remaining (lower-risk) groups was not established and the authors state it “needs further assessment.”
  • Because HOXB-superfamily categories (NUP98r, UBTF, KMT2A-PTD, DEK::NUP214, NPM1) show high co-occurrence of FLT3-ITD, the authors suggest FLT3 inhibitor treatment could be effective in FLT3-ITD+ HOXB subtypes independent of the FLT3-ITD allelic ratio — a hypothesis raised in the Discussion, not tested prospectively here.
  • Given that KMT2Ar, NUP98r and NPM1 AMLs are known to depend on the menin–KMT2A interaction and are being targeted by menin inhibitors in clinical trials, and citing the authors’ own prior finding that UBTF-mutated AML is sensitive to menin inhibitors, the paper proposes that other HOX-expressing categories may also be candidates for menin-inhibitor therapy — again a proposed hypothesis rather than a result generated in this study.
  • The recommended diagnostic strategy is RNA-seq-based (rather than targeted DNA panels), because it can detect cryptic and canonical fusions, outlier/allele-specific expression signatures (used to call MECOM, BCL11B and MNX1 categories) and provides sufficient mutation sensitivity — the authors note existing commercial DNA panels for pAML often lack coverage of key defining genes such as UBTF or cannot detect the structural variants that drive MECOM/BCL11B overexpression.
  • Sorafenib, used per AAML1031 protocol in patients with high-allelic-ratio FLT3-ITD, is cited as a likely confounder that attenuated the prognostic significance of age and FLT3-ITD status in the AAML1031 univariate survival analysis.

Limitations & open questions

  • The discovery cohort aggregates samples and sequencing data from multiple prior studies plus newly generated St. Jude data (RNA-seq for all 887, orthogonal WGS/WES for only 74.9%); classification for the remaining ~25% of cases rests on RNA-seq alone, cross-validated at 97.3% concordance against WGS/WES calls in the subset where both were available, but not verified case-by-case throughout the whole cohort.
  • 9 of the 76 “Unclassified” cases had no pathogenic alteration detected at all, partly attributable to the lack of WGS data for 8 of these cases — a data-completeness limitation rather than a biological finding.
  • Within KMT2Ar and NUP98r/NPM1/UBTF categories, both fusion partner and expression-cluster membership were independently prognostic in Cox models, but the difference in predictive power between the two representations was not statistically resolved (95% bootstrap C-index interval −0.025 to 0.093), leaving open which explains outcome heterogeneity better.
  • The clinical benefit of transplantation in first remission was demonstrated only for high-risk, MRD-positive categories; the authors explicitly flag that benefit in the remaining risk/MRD strata “needs further assessment.”
  • Rare, single-patient candidate drivers (MLLT10 fusions, RUNX1::USP42/EVX1/ZEB2, JAK2 p.V617F, MLLT1 p.C119SPAR, H3-3A p.K28M) are proposed by analogy to other pediatric cancers but the authors state these “require a larger cohort for further categorization” before being accepted as disease-defining.
  • The framework depends on RNA-seq-based sequencing and category-specific expertise; the authors acknowledge that “clinical sequencing is not readily available globally,” which could limit adoption outside specialized centers.
  • The paper explicitly notes it used no custom code or software beyond published, cited tools (CREST, GISTIC, WGCNA, GSEA, rpart, survival), which aids reproducibility but means algorithm choices were not benchmarked against alternatives within this study.
